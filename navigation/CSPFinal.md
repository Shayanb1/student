# A Year of Building: What This CS Class Really Taught Me

When I started this class, I was writing code to make a Snake game run in a browser.
By the end, I was standing in front of library executives presenting a full-stack
web platform I built with my team. That gap is what this year was all about.

## Trimester 1: Learning to Think Like a Developer

It started small. Variables, booleans, conditionals, lists. Python one day,
JavaScript the next. I built simple games like Snake, Tic Tac Toe, Flappy Bird,
and GeoDash just to get the fundamentals down. At the time it felt like busy work
but looking back, those foundations mattered more than I realized.

By the end of trimester 1 I was writing Flask APIs and working with async
JavaScript. The jump from "variables and loops" to "here's a backend endpoint
that returns data" happened faster than I expected, and that momentum carried
into everything that came next.

## Trimester 2: First Real Project

My first real team project was **Moodlife**, a mood and wellness tracking platform.
This is where things got serious.

I built a group chat system, a direct messaging feature, a full admin panel,
and a facial expression recognition module using face-api.js that could detect
your mood through your webcam and log it automatically. That last feature is
still one of my favorites because it combined something technically interesting
with something genuinely useful.

Working in a group for the first time was an adjustment. You have to communicate,
split work properly, and trust people to deliver. It did not always go smoothly
but we shipped a real product, and I came out of it a better collaborator.

## Trimester 3: The Capstone

The final project was the one that changed my perspective on what this class
is really preparing us for.

Our team rebuilt the Friends of the Poway Library website from the ground up.
It went live at fopl.opencodingsociety.com. I built the core of it: the events
calendar, volunteer portal, book scanner with USB barcode support, admin
dashboard, authentication system, and multiple games including a facial
recognition game and Library Dodge.

The part I will not forget is the presentation. Executives from the Friends of
the Poway Library came to our school: Bob Masters, Kim Niehans, Mark Reyes,
everything we built. It was not a classroom demo. It was a real pitch to real
stakeholders, and they were genuinely engaged with what we made.

That experience taught me something no tutorial ever could. Building something
is only half of it. Being able to explain it, defend your decisions, and make
someone who is not a developer care about what you made is the other half.

## What I'm Taking Away

I came into this year as someone who could write code but struggled to present
and communicate. I'm leaving it with a much clearer picture of how software
actually gets built and shipped in the real world: in teams, under pressure,
for people outside the classroom.

The tools I learned here (Flask, Jekyll, Docker, APIs, JWT auth, Git) are not
just class assignments. They are the exact things I will use if I pursue
software engineering seriously. And honestly, after this year, I want to.

This class gave me proof that I can build things people actually use. That
means more to me than any grade.
